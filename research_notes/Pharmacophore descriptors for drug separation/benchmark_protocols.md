# Benchmark protocols, metrics and similarity significance for judging how well a descriptor separates drugs

Scope: how academia and industry check that a fingerprint or descriptor ranks related drugs above unrelated ones, and how a similarity value is given statistical meaning. Everything below is paraphrased. Numbers come from the cited source unless the text says otherwise. Research date: 2026-11-03.

## (a) Benchmark datasets and evaluation protocols (ligand-based VS, drug-class / target retrieval)

### Takeaway
The standard 2D-fingerprint protocol (Riniker & Landrum 2013) works like this. Take a few known actives as queries, rank a pool of actives plus decoys or inactives by MAX-fused similarity, repeat over many random query draws and many targets, and compare methods with rank-based statistics. Every dataset family carries biases. DUD/DUD-E are too easy (analog bias, decoys topologically unlike actives). MUV and LIT-PCBA are hard but noisy, and LIT-PCBA has leakage issues of its own. The O'Boyle & Sayle literature benchmark asks a different question (does the fingerprint order analogues the way medicinal chemists do?) and uses no decoys. No standard published ATC-class retrieval benchmark turned up. The closest precedent is SuperPred's leave-one-out nearest-neighbour ATC assignment.

### Cited Findings
**Riniker & Landrum 2013 benchmarking platform** (J Cheminf 5:26, DOI 10.1186/1758-2946-5-26)
- 88 targets from three sources. DUD: 21 of 40 targets, those with >30 actives (4 to 365 actives each, property-matched ZINC decoys). MUV: 17 targets, 30 actives + 15,000 decoys each. ChEMBL: 50 human targets (ChEMBL 14, potency ≤10 µM). The ChEMBL sets use the 100 most diverse actives per target plus 10,000 ZINC decoys shared across targets, two per active, chosen with ECFC0 Dice >0.5 so that they are not trivially different — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- Protocol: 5 query actives per run, 50 random repetitions per target. The "training" list holds the 5 queries plus 20% of decoys, and the remaining actives and decoys form the test set. Each test molecule is scored by its maximum similarity to any query (MAX fusion). Dice is the default measure, and the authors prove Tanimoto and Dice give identical rankings — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- 14 fingerprints, including two baselines (ECFC0, MACCS). Metrics: AUC, EF at 1% and 5%, RIE and BEDROC at α=20 and α=100 — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- Results: a global Friedman test was significant for every metric (p < 2.2e-16). Post-hoc pairwise tests (91 pairs, 100 bootstrap resamples, max-T multiplicity adjustment, α=0.05) showed that, apart from the baselines, fingerprints were largely indistinguishable. Topological torsion (1987) ranked near the top under every metric. Under AUC, path-based fingerprints (TT, AP) beat circular ones. Under BEDROC(20), long ECFP4/ECFP6 beat path-based ones. The baselines lost significantly on early-recognition metrics — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- Biases they observed. DUD: several targets (DHFR, ER_agonist, GR, SAHH) reach AUC > 0.95 even with MACCS, so they are too easy. MUV: near-random or worse than random for most targets, with only PKA, cathepsin G, FXIa and FXIIa showing enrichment. The active/decoy ratio strongly changes the maximum attainable RIE and EF(1%) between targets. A "scaffold EF" (scaffold-hopping) analysis tracked overall performance closely and added little — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)

**O'Boyle & Sayle 2016 literature-based benchmark** (J Cheminf 8:36, DOI 10.1186/s13321-016-0148-0)
- Premise: compounds that a medicinal chemistry paper reports together in one activity table were judged similar by its authors. The benchmarks are built from ChEMBL 20 co-occurrence and use no decoys — [PMC4932683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4932683/)
- Single-assay benchmark: ≥4,563 series of 5 molecules from one assay, ordered by activity relative to the reference. It tests close analogues. Multi-assay benchmark: ≥3,629 series of 5 molecules that chain across papers sharing a compound, so similarity to the reference falls along the series. It tests more diverse structures. Each benchmark is built 1,000 times with shuffling — [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4932683/fullTextXML)
- Metric: Spearman correlation between the fingerprint's ranking of members 2–5 and the benchmark order. For each pair of fingerprints, the net count of series won is computed per repetition. The 1,000 net differences are tested with a two-sided t-test at 1%, with Holm–Bonferroni correction over 378 comparisons — [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4932683/fullTextXML)
- Results over 28 RDKit fingerprints: long ECFP4/ECFP6 and topological torsion were best on the multi-assay set. Atom pairs (HashAP) were best on the single-assay set. ECFP0, RDK7, ECFC0 and MACCS were poor, and the authors advise against Daylight-type path fingerprints and MACCS. Count vectors helped on single-assay but hurt on multi-assay and VS. 16,384-bit beat 1,024-bit on multi-assay (e.g. net difference of 172 for LECFP6 vs ECFP6). Rank agreement (Kendall τ) with the Riniker–Landrum VS benchmark was 0.68 for multi-assay and only 0.35 for single-assay. Re-analysed with their method, 35 of the 91 VS comparisons were not significant — [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4932683/fullTextXML)

**MUV (Rohrer & Baumann 2009)** (J Chem Inf Model 49(2):169–184)
- 17 PubChem-derived targets, 30 actives + 15,000 decoys each. The sets were designed with spatial-statistics (refined nearest-neighbour) analysis to remove analog bias and artificial enrichment, and the data and design software are freely available — [bibbase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/rohrer-baumann-maximumunbiasedvalidationmuvdatasetsforvirtualscreeningbasedonpubchembioactivitydata-2009); [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- Caveat: with random train/validation splits, MUV validation inactives resemble training inactives, so ML models can score well by learning the negatives (Wallach & Heifets AVE analysis) — [arXiv 1706.06619](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.06619)

**DUD / DUD-E**
- DUD-E decoys are property-matched to actives but chosen to be topologically dissimilar. Sieg et al. 2019 showed that deep-learning VS performance on DUD-E comes largely from analogue bias and decoy bias rather than from learned binding physics — [Sieg et al. 2019, PLOS ONE, PMC6701836](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6701836/)
- A follow-up analysis found that stricter property matching reduces property bias, but a topology bias remains because fingerprint dissimilarity was itself a decoy-selection criterion. This favours 2D-fingerprint methods by construction — [PMC7052818](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052818) (authorship and year not verified)
- DeepCoy generates decoys with tighter property matching for DUD-E targets — [PMC8352508](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8352508/)

**LIT-PCBA (Tran-Nguyen, Jacquemard & Rognan 2020)** (J Chem Inf Model 60(9):4263–4273)
- Built from 149 dose-response PubChem bioassays, filtered for artefacts and false positives, with actives and inactives kept in the same property ranges. Of 21 candidate sets, 15 targets were kept: those where at least one of three methods gave EF1% ≥ 2. The sets were then unbiased with AVE. The paper reports 7,844 confirmed actives and 407,381 confirmed inactives. The project site lists 7,761 / 407,839 and ESR_ago with only 13 actives — [PubMed 32282202](https://www.pubmed.gov/32282202/); [LIT-PCBA site](https://drugdesign.unistra.fr/LIT-PCBA/index.htm)
- 2025 audit: 2,491 2D-identical inactives appear in both training and validation splits, so some high reported enrichments may be inflated — [arXiv 2507.21404](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21404v2.pdf)

**Drug-class (ATC) and target retrieval**
- SuperPred (2008): 1,035 drugs (single indication, ATC groups with ≥3 members), CDK fingerprint, Tanimoto, nearest-neighbour ATC assignment, leave-one-out. Overall accuracy was 67.6% (700/1,035). Accuracy rose with the top-hit Tanimoto: 21.7% at 0.4–0.5, 40% at 0.5–0.7, 52.5% at 0.7–0.8, 74.7% at 0.8–0.9 and 82.3% at 0.9–1.0, with ~81% above 0.85 (the abstract says 81%, the results 80.6%). Cumulative recall was ~80% within the top 3 retrieved drugs and ~90% within the top 20 — [SuperPred, PMC2447784](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2447784/)
- ATC prediction work has encoded each drug as an 881-bit PubChem substructure fingerprint and aggregated pairwise Tanimoto scores into ATC-to-ATC similarity, then predicted new ATC codes from that — [Chen et al., J Biomed Inform](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1532046415002105)
- Target-retrieval benchmarking (the analogue of drug-class retrieval) is usually done as in the SEA follow-up: train on one ChEMBL release and test on ligand-target pairs new in the next release. The improved-SEA paper used ChEMBL 19 for training and a ChEMBL 20 test set of 37,138 positive and 42,928 negative ligand-target interactions, scored with accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity and F-beta (F0.5, F0.25, which weight precision) — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)

### Inferences
- Benchmark choice decides the answer. Fingerprint rankings from the multi-assay literature benchmark and the VS benchmark agree only moderately (Kendall tau 0.68), and the close-analogue single-assay benchmark agrees with VS at tau 0.35. A custom pharmacophore vector should therefore be measured on at least two task families (decoy-based retrieval and analogue ordering), because a win on one does not transfer.
- A pharmacophore descriptor is at a structural disadvantage on DUD-E-style sets, because DUD-E decoys were explicitly selected to be topologically unlike actives, which rewards 2D-topological fingerprints. LIT-PCBA and MUV are fairer but much harder, and LIT-PCBA needs its own de-duplication check before splits.
- For the Pharmagen use case (ranking related drugs above unrelated ones), the ATC/target-class retrieval protocol has to be built in-house: pick query drugs, rank the rest by similarity, and report top-k retrieval of same-class drugs, with the SuperPred leave-one-out nearest-neighbour design as the precedent.

### Gaps
- No standard, widely cited ATC-code retrieval benchmark with published per-fingerprint numbers was found. Published ATC work is prediction-oriented, not fingerprint-comparison-oriented.
- DEKOIS (Bauer, Vogt, Bajorath et al.) was not retrieved in full. Its design (property-matched decoys from ZINC with a latent-actives-in-decoys check, DEKOIS 2.0 covering 81 targets) could not be verified from a primary source in this session, so treat those details as unconfirmed.
- The original Truchon & Bayly 2007 and Rohrer & Baumann 2009 full texts were not retrieved (paywalled). Their details below come from abstracts and secondary citations, flagged where that is the case.

## (b) Evaluation metrics and their pitfalls

### Takeaway
Report global ranking quality (ROC AUC) and early recognition (BEDROC or ROC enrichment at fixed false-positive rates) together, never the classical enrichment factor alone, because EF mixes a property of the method with a property of the experimental set-up. Compare methods with rank-based, multiplicity-corrected statistics over many targets and many random query draws, and always show error bars.

### Cited Findings
- Jain & Nicholls recommend reporting AUC plus early enrichment at four thresholds (0.5%, 1%, 2%, 5%), defined as the ratio of true-positive rate to false-positive rate at that threshold. Expressing it that way removes dependence on the active-to-inactive ratio and, in their words, "makes standard statistical analysis of error bars much simpler" — [Jain & Nicholls 2008, J Comput Aided Mol Des 22:133, Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC2311385/fullTextXML)
- The same paper notes AUC is a global measure that can miss early enrichment, particularly when AUC is below roughly 0.8, and that classical enrichment reflects "a property of a method and an experimental set-up" rather than the method alone, calling it "by nearly all other considerations a poor measure" — [Jain & Nicholls 2008](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC2311385/fullTextXML)
- On reporting discipline, Jain & Nicholls call the absence of error bars and significance testing "the most lamentable aspect of reporting in our field" and require proper statistical validation before any superiority claim. They give no formula for AUC confidence intervals, only citations — [Jain & Nicholls 2008](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC2311385/fullTextXML)
- BEDROC (Truchon & Bayly 2007, J Chem Inf Model 47(2):488–508, DOI 10.1021/ci600426e) weights actives by an exponential decay in rank. At the recommended alpha = 20, about 80% of the maximum achievable score comes from the top 8% of the ranked list. The paper's two headline error sources are statistical error and saturation effects, and it gives recommendations for choosing the numbers of actives and inactives and the value of alpha — [paper abstract record](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2042110087); [Front Chem 2019 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/chemistry/articles/10.3389/fchem.2019.00701/full)
- BEDROC and RIE are statistically equivalent metrics, and overemphasising early recognition costs statistical power (a "seesaw effect": metrics that look earlier are noisier) — [Zhao et al. 2009, BMC Bioinformatics 10:225](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/1471-2105-10-225)
- Practical consequence of EF's ratio dependence: in the Riniker & Landrum platform, the maximum attainable RIE and EF(1%) differ between DUD targets because the actives-to-decoys ratio differs, so cross-target averages of those metrics are not clean. They advise choosing metric parameters carefully — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/)
- Multi-target, multi-repetition comparison protocols in use: Friedman global test plus post-hoc pairwise Friedman tests over 91 fingerprint pairs with 100 bootstrap resamples and max-T multiplicity adjustment at alpha = 0.05 (Riniker & Landrum); and per-repetition net-win counts over 1,000 benchmark rebuilds tested with a two-sided t-test at 1% plus Holm-Bonferroni over 378 pairs (O'Boyle & Sayle) — [PMC3686626](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686626/); [PMC4932683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4932683/)
- Both of those studies found most fingerprint pairs statistically indistinguishable once multiplicity is handled: 46 of 91 pairs non-significant at 5% in the original VS analysis, 35 of 91 in O'Boyle & Sayle's re-analysis — [PMC4932683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4932683/)
- For ordering tasks (is a related drug ranked above a less related one), Spearman rank correlation of the predicted order against a reference order is the metric used, with pairwise net-win counts as the aggregation — [PMC4932683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4932683/)
- For target/class prediction framed as classification, the improved-SEA study reported accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity and F-beta rather than AUC, and the precision/sensitivity trade-off was severe: a strict 0.1 uM activity model gave 95.8% precision but 7.2% sensitivity, while a 10 uM model gave 67.6% accuracy and 38.2% sensitivity — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- Fingerprint comparison on that target-prediction task (at p <= 0.05) ranked atom pair approximately equal to topological > Morgan > MACCS > 2D pharmacophore. Topological reached 83.7% precision and F0.25 of 0.784. A 2-point pharmacophore fingerprint gave only 47.9% precision, too fuzzy to build a model on, and SHED descriptors gave 45.4% — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- Fingerprint similarity search is reviewed as most valuable exactly when few, unrelated ligands are known for a target, since richer pharmacophore or structure-based methods are then unavailable — [Muegge & Mukherjee 2016, Expert Opin Drug Discov, record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100031846621_0x000001)

### Inferences
- A defensible metric panel for a custom pharmacophore vector: ROC AUC (global), BEDROC at alpha 20 and 100 (early recognition, matching the Riniker & Landrum setting so results are comparable to the published table), ROC enrichment at 0.5/1/2/5% FPR (ratio-independent, per Jain & Nicholls), and top-k retrieval rate for the class-retrieval framing. EF should appear only alongside the actives:decoys ratio that produced it.
- Because BEDROC and RIE are equivalent, reporting both adds nothing. Reporting AUC and one early-recognition metric covers the two distinct failure modes.
- Given the seesaw effect, an early-recognition metric needs more repetitions (50 query draws per target in the published protocol) to reach the same statistical power as AUC.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved that specifically recommends PRC-AUC (average precision) for ligand-based similarity benchmarking. It is standard practice for imbalanced retrieval generally, but no cheminformatics-specific citation with numbers was found in this session. The ratio-independent ROC-enrichment recommendation from Jain & Nicholls is the better-sourced alternative.
- No explicit formula for AUC standard error or confidence intervals was found in the retrieved texts. Bootstrap over query draws (as both benchmark papers do) is the method actually used in practice, not an analytic interval.

## (c) Giving a similarity value statistical significance

### Takeaway
Raw similarity values have no absolute meaning and are not comparable across descriptors. Significance is established by building a background distribution of similarities between unrelated pairs or random sets, then expressing an observed score as a percentile, a Z-score, or a database-size-corrected E-value. The two canonical recipes are percentile thresholds from random-pair distributions per fingerprint (Landrum; Jasial/Hu/Bajorath) and the SEA set-level Z-score / extreme-value E-value model (Keiser et al.).

### Cited Findings
**Random-pair percentile thresholds (per-fingerprint noise levels)**
- Landrum's method: take 50,000 ChEMBL 35 molecule pairs (single fragment, under 50 heavy atoms), shuffle the second member of each pair so the pairs are random, compute Tanimoto for each fingerprint, sort, and read off the 70/80/90/95/99th percentile. The 99th percentile is the value a random pair exceeds only 1% of the time — [RDKit blog, similarity thresholds](https://greglandrum.github.io/rdkit-blog/posts/2021-05-18-fingerprint-thresholds1.html)
- Selected 95% / 99% thresholds from that run, showing how far apart fingerprints are: MACCS 0.592 / 0.667; Morgan2 counts 0.248 / 0.297; Morgan2 bits 0.185 / 0.226; Morgan3 bits 0.141 / 0.173; FeatMorgan2 counts 0.270 / 0.319; Atom Pairs counts 0.266 / 0.320; Atom Pairs bits 0.371 / 0.419; Topological Torsions counts 0.188 / 0.256; RDKit7 counts 0.133 / 0.201; Avalon bits 0.515 / 0.589; Pattern 0.698 / 0.750; Gobbi 2D pharmacophore 0.292 / 0.373 — [RDKit blog](https://greglandrum.github.io/rdkit-blog/posts/2021-05-18-fingerprint-thresholds1.html)
- Caveats stated there: the choice of percentile is subjective (low enough not to lose true neighbours, high enough to exclude noise), the answer depends heavily on fingerprint details, these are percentiles of one ChEMBL-derived sample rather than universal constants, and the post was corrected after an earlier version mistakenly used count-based folded fingerprints for the bit-based Morgan/FeatMorgan rows, inflating those thresholds — [RDKit blog](https://greglandrum.github.io/rdkit-blog/posts/2021-05-18-fingerprint-thresholds1.html)
- The spread (0.185 for Morgan2 bits versus 0.698 for the Pattern fingerprint at the same 95% noise level, and 0.292 for a 2D pharmacophore fingerprint) is the direct demonstration that a raw similarity value cannot be interpreted or compared without its own background distribution.

**Activity-relevant thresholds from active-vs-active versus random distributions**
- Jasial, Hu & Bajorath built 102 ChEMBL 20 activity classes (at least 50 compounds, human targets, potency at least 10 uM, MW up to 550): 22 "easy" (2,967 compounds), 50 "intermediate" (25,175) and 30 "difficult" (47,109), with 10,000 ZINC compounds as assumed inactives. They compared active-vs-active, random-vs-random and active-vs-random distributions (around 753 million active-vs-random Tanimoto values, 50 million sampled) — [Jasial, Hu & Bajorath 2016, F1000Research 5:591](https://f1000research.com/articles/5-591)
- Thresholds derived where active-vs-active diverges from the random baseline: Tanimoto at least 0.8 for MACCS and at least 0.3 for ECFP4. Distribution means: MACCS random baseline about 0.4 versus 0.47 for actives; ECFP4 baseline about 0.11 versus 0.15 for actives; for easy classes the active means were 0.6 (MACCS) and 0.28 (ECFP4) — [F1000Research 5:591](https://f1000research.com/articles/5-591)
- Fraction of pairs above those thresholds, active-vs-active versus active-vs-random: easy classes, MACCS 16.0% versus 0.005% and ECFP4 38.2% versus 0.03%; intermediate, MACCS 5.0% versus 0.01% and ECFP4 10.9% versus 0.04%; difficult, MACCS 1.2% versus 0.01% and ECFP4 4.1% versus 0.05% — [F1000Research 5:591](https://f1000research.com/articles/5-591)
- Their warning about using such thresholds as search cutoffs: with one reference compound on an easy class, MACCS at 0.8 against 100,000 inactives and 50 actives yields roughly 8 true and 5 false positives, and the false positives rise to about 25 at 500,000 inactives; ECFP4 at 0.3 against 500,000 inactives and 50 actives yields roughly 19 true but about 150 false positives. Even on easy classes ECFP4 recovered under 40% of actives at its activity-relevant threshold, so the authors conclude these values are not reliable search thresholds — [F1000Research 5:591](https://f1000research.com/articles/5-591)
- The practical implication they draw is that most active compounds of a class are not structurally similar to each other by fingerprint standards, and that the signal lives in a thin tail of the distribution.

**Set-level significance: SEA Z-scores and E-values**
- SEA (Keiser et al. 2007, Nat Biotechnol, DOI 10.1038/nbt1284) compares two ligand sets rather than two molecules. The raw score is the sum of all pairwise Tanimoto coefficients that meet a threshold TS, with sub-threshold pairs contributing zero — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- The raw score grows with set size, so it is normalised against random sets. The improved-SEA reimplementation fits mean and standard deviation as functions of s, the product of the two set sizes: mean = mu * s (linear) and SD = phi * s^eta (power law). Then Z = (raw score - mean(s)) / SD(s) — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- The background was built by drawing random sets of sizes 10 to 1000 in steps of 10 (4,950 set pairs) from curated ChEMBL 19 data, repeated 100 times — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- Z-scores are converted to a p-value through an extreme value distribution, with a Taylor expansion used above Z = 28 to avoid numerical overflow. The E-value is the p-value multiplied by the database size, E = p * N_db, i.e. the number of comparisons of that quality expected by chance; an E-value of 1 or more is not significant, and values below about 1e-10 are treated empirically as very strong — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/); [Bioinformatics 27(21):3044](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/27/21/3044/217204)
- TS is not guessed. Raw scores are computed for every threshold from 0.00 to 0.99 in 0.01 steps, and the threshold chosen is the one whose Z-score distribution best fits the extreme value distribution by a chi-square test. For the Morgan fingerprint the fitted TS was 0.69 at the 0.1 uM and 1 uM activity cutoffs and 0.62 at 10 uM — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/)
- Significance cutoffs used in practice there: p <= 0.05 for the main analysis, p <= 0.01 as a stricter variant, and p <= 1e-80 for network visualisation. As a scale reference, the original paper reports a DHFR ligand set compared with itself at Z = 333.4 and expectation value 7.07e-182 — [PMC4842302](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842302/); [Keiser et al. related PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2914517)

**The 0.85 folklore**
- The often-quoted "Tanimoto above 0.85 means similar activity" rule is fingerprint-specific and weakly supported. SuperPred cites it and its own data show only about 81% correct ATC assignment above 0.85, falling to 52.5% in the 0.7-0.8 band and about 40% between 0.5 and 0.7 — [SuperPred, PMC2447784](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2447784/)
- Landrum's percentile table makes the incompatibility explicit: 0.85 is far above the 99th percentile of random pairs for Morgan2 (0.226 for bits) yet below it for the Pattern fingerprint (0.750), so one number cannot serve both — [RDKit blog](https://greglandrum.github.io/rdkit-blog/posts/2021-05-18-fingerprint-thresholds1.html)
- Quantitative similarity between marketed drugs varies markedly with the encoding used, which is why consensus rank orderings across fingerprints have been proposed instead of single-fingerprint cutoffs — [O'Hagan & Kell, bioRxiv 110437](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/110437.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible significance procedure for a custom pharmacophore vector, assembled from the above: (1) build a background by pairing randomly shuffled molecules from the working library (Landrum used 50,000 pairs) and record the 90/95/99th percentiles for the chosen similarity measure; (2) report any observed similarity as a percentile or Z-score against that background, never as a raw number; (3) if the question is set-level (does this drug relate to this class or target), use the SEA construction, fitting mean and SD against the product of set sizes and converting to an E-value so the database size is accounted for; (4) re-derive thresholds whenever the vector definition, the weighting, or the library changes.
- Because the pharmacophore vector will be a continuous or count vector rather than a bit string, the Tanimoto/Dice equivalence proof and the published bit-string thresholds do not transfer. The background distribution must be recomputed for the exact metric used (Dice on counts, cosine, or Euclidean-derived similarity as in SHED).
- The SHED (45.4% precision) and 2-point pharmacophore (47.9%) results are a warning that a low-dimensional, highly fuzzy pharmacophore representation can be too coarse to discriminate at all. Dimensionality and fuzziness should be treated as tunable and validated on the same benchmark, not fixed by design intuition.
- Threshold-based retrieval and rank-based evaluation answer different questions. Jasial et al. show that a threshold tuned to be statistically distinguishable from random is still useless as a practical search cutoff at library scale, because false positives scale with library size while true positives do not. Ranking metrics (AUC, BEDROC, top-k) should carry the main evaluation, with thresholds reported only with their expected false-positive count at a stated library size.

### Gaps
- Baldi & Nasr's analytic theory of Tanimoto score distributions and extreme values (which derives Z-scores, p-values and E-values for single-pair similarities rather than set similarities) was identified as the relevant companion theory but was not retrieved in full, so no formulas or numbers from it are reported here.
- The original Keiser et al. 2007 and 2009 full texts could not be fetched (403 and 500 errors from the mirrors tried). The SEA model details above come from the improved-SEA reimplementation (PMC4842302), which states it follows the original "with minor changes", plus a secondary record for the DHFR Z-score example. The 2007 paper's own fingerprint and TS value were not verified directly.
- No source was found that reports background-distribution thresholds for 3D or pharmacophore-feature vectors specifically, beyond the Gobbi 2D pharmacophore fingerprint row (95%: 0.292, 99%: 0.373) in Landrum's table. For a custom pharmacophore vector, there appears to be no published reference threshold to borrow.
